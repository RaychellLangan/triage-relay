Triage Relay

A patient's messy description goes in. A ward gets a short, prebaked card: which department, how urgent, how sure, and what to ask next. When the system is out of its depth it says so and hands the case to a person. It never names a diagnosis.

How it works

Six steps, each one inspectable

A small transformer does the one thing transformers are good at: turning loose language into a fixed vocabulary. Everything that decides is counted and readable, so a clinician can see why a card says what it says.

1TranslatorA 6-million-parameter transformer reads the messy words and writes the patient's answers as fixed fields. It cannot write anything else.
2Counted seesawsEach field pushes on a plank for every department and condition. The pushes are counted from example patients, not learned by training, and every one can be read.
3Danger firstA separate engine asks what could hurt the patient soonest. If a severe condition is still in play, the card routes for it.
4Second readerA second transformer reads the original text and gives an independent opinion, which matters most where the seesaws are torn.
5VerdictAnother seesaw weighs the evidence: is it clear, do the readers agree, does this case look like anything seen before?
6Ward cardA prebaked template filled from data. Every word is a slot; nothing is generated.

Knowing what it does not know

A second sense for new situations

Reading the middle of the model

Before it answers, the second reader's middle layers are compared with everything it has seen before. A case that sits far from all of them gets marked as new. On the cases the counted seesaws get wrong, this signal picks out the wrong cards 81–87% of the time (AUROC 0.81–0.87), where the seesaws' own alarm is no better than chance. It also adds to a plain "how much did the patient tell us" baseline, so it is not just counting answers.

Learning from people, without retraining

When a person corrects a card, the correction is remembered with the case. The next look-alike case shows it, with a closeness score that says how far to trust it: in the closest quarter, remembered answers were right every time. A reviewer decides whether a correction stays with that one case or becomes a rule for everyone, and can see exactly which answers the rule would key on.

Safety

How it handles the things that matter

The brief asksWhat Triage Relay does
Wrong or unsupported answersRoutes only when the evidence is clear, the two readers agree and the case looks familiar. Otherwise it hands over. Out-of-place text (an ordinary paragraph, a scrambled intake) is not routed.
Uncertain resultsEvery card shows its verdict (yes, hand to a person, no, no idea), the runner-up departments and how close they came, and which facts made it unsure.
High-risk decisionsA separate danger engine routes for the most serious condition still in play, so a possible heart problem is not sent to a slower queue because something milder was more likely.
Human reviewCases it is unsure of go to a person with the questions that would settle them. Corrections are logged with who made them; a reviewer approves any wider rule.
Sensitive informationRuns locally on small models; nothing is sent anywhere. All development and testing used synthetic patients (DDXPlus), never real records.
DiagnosisIt never names a condition to anyone. It suggests where a person should be seen and how soon.
